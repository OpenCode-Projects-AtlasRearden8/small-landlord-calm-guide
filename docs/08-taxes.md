# Chapter 8 — Taxes, in Round Numbers

This chapter answers "what does renting a room or a house actually mean for my
taxes" — with round numbers, on purpose. Tax rules move around; the *shape*
of the answer mostly doesn't. Understand the shape, then confirm the numbers
with the IRS instructions in question and a CPA the first year you file.

> Education only, absolutely not tax advice. GST-style VAT jokes aside, this
> is U.S.-shaped guidance for a small landlord. Numbers are illustrative,
> rounded, and may not match any particular tax year.

## #1: Rental income is income

Money you receive for letting someone live in your property is taxable income,
on top of your nursing wages. If you rented a place for $1,500 a month for the
full year, that's about **$18,000 of rental income on your tax return**, on a
separate schedule (Schedule E) attached to Form 1040.

## #2: The second half of the story: expenses offset income

You don't pay tax on the whole $18,000. You subtract the ordinary business
expenses — which is the entire intellectual joy of being a landlord (modest,
but real):

- repairs (not upgrades — see below), cleaning, supplies
- insurance premiums for the rental policy
- property taxes
- interest on the mortgage for the rental property
- fees you paid (screening service, accountant, attorney)
- utilities you paid as the landlord
- advertising the listing
- driving across town for a repair (mileage, at the current-year rate — look
  up the IRS's standard mileage rate for the year)
- depreciation (the big one — next)

Round number example, same $18,000 of income:

| Line | Round number |
|---|---|
| Rental income | $18,000 |
| Insurance | −$1,500 |
| Property tax | −$3,000 |
| Mortgage interest | −$6,500 |
| Repairs and supplies | −$1,200 |
| Screening/service fees | −$200 |
| **Net** | **$5,600** of taxable rental income |

You'd owe ordinary income tax on that $5,600 (at your rate), plus possibly a
self-employment question — which for plain rental income is normally *not*
applied (rental income usually isn't self-employment income, unless you're
providing substantial services — a CPA nails this). Confirm with real
instructions each year; this chapter's job is the shape.

## #3: Depreciation, without the panic

Houses "wear out" on paper, says the tax code. You get to subtract a fraction
of the building's cost over many years — commonly about 27.5 years for a
residential rental.

Round-number example: you bought a house for $200,000, and county records say
$150,000 of that was the building (land doesn't depreciate — ask a CPA how to
make that split). $150,000 ÷ 28 ≈ **$5,400/year** paper deduction.

Yes, that's a real deduction that reduces your taxable rental income without
your writing a check to anyone. It is also the reason you keep records of the
purchase price and closing costs instead of throwing out the closing packet.

Watch one thing: **partial-year years** ("mid-month convention" is the phrase
your software will whisper) and the fact that the first year you rented —
with an ADU you built, for example — starts when the rental was *placed in
service* (ready and offered for rent), not when a tenant eventually moved in.

## #4: Repairs vs. improvements (this one matters)

The tax code splits them:

- **Repair** — returning something to working condition: a new garbage
  disposal, patching a section of roof, repainting a scuffed wall, a new
  water heater. Deducted **this year**.
- **Improvement** — making it *better* than it was: adding a bathroom,
  finishing the ADU's closet into a kitchenette, replacing all the windows
  with nicer ones, replacing a roof (harsh, but generally improvement-shaped).
  These are **capitalized** — added to the building's depreciable basis —
  and deducted slowly, over years.

Same $1,500 of spending either way; the *timing* differs by years. Keep the
invoices labeled, and ask "is this making it better, or making it work
again?" when you file each January.

## #5: Renting a room *in your own home* — the special case

Two things happen:

1. You're renting a *portion* of your home, so you split the expenses
   pro-rata. If the rented room is about 15% of the house, roughly:
   15% of the mortgage interest, 15% of the property tax, 15% of the
   utilities, 15% of depreciation on the *house* portion — and you don't pay
   tax on the other 85% that was always yours anyway.
2. If you rent a room for **fewer than 15 days in a year** (the occasional
   short-term houseguest-airbnb case), that income is generally **not
   taxable** — and the related expenses generally **aren't deductible**
   either. Look up the "Masters rule" if this is you; but know the rule
   exists and what its 15-day threshold is.

## #6: 1099s and who reports what to whom

- You generally don't 1099 a tenant (they pay *you* rent).
- If you pay a contractor $600 or more in a year (a handyperson, a real
  property manager, a lawyer, eventually), you generally need to give **them**
  (and the IRS) a Form 1099-NEC, unless they're a corporation. Keep their W-9
  *before* you pay them, not after. Get into the habit: *"W-9 before work
  starts, please — it's just paperwork on my end."*
- If you paid a property management company rent-management fees, they're
  deductible expenses, and they'll 1099 *you* for rent you received if they
  collected it.

## #7: Selling the rental (Chapter 9 talks about *whether*)

When you sell:

- The **gain** — what you sold for minus your adjusted basis (original price
  plus improvements minus depreciation *taken*) — is taxable.
- Because depreciation lowered your basis, some of the gain may be
  "unrecaptured" at a special (up to 25%) rate. Ask a CPA; the round-number
  version is: *depreciation you deducted comes back when you sell, at ~25%.*
- If it was your **main home** at some point, there are exclusions (the
  famous "lived 2 of last 5 years" rule) that may cover part of the gain —
  with rental-use periods treated specially. Definitely a pro conversation.

## #8: Three recordkeeping things that pay for the CPA

1. **Save every invoice** for repairs and improvements; label
   "repair" or "improvement" at the moment you pay.
2. **Keep the closing packet** from when you bought (it has your basis).
3. **Log expenses monthly** (Chapter 7's fifteen-minute habit), so tax season
   is reading off a list, not archaeology.

A CPA or enrolled agent costs in the ballpark of $300–$800 for a small-landlord
return with one Schedule E and maybe a state return — which is cheap insurance
against an expensive mistake the first year you file, especially if
depreciation, a home-office, an ADU, and a partial-year are all tangled in
there together. After the first year, the return is mostly a copy, and many
people go back to filing it themselves.

## Round-numbers summary card

| Thing you'll wonder | Round-number answer |
|---|---|
| Is rental income taxable? | Yes, on Schedule E |
| What do I subtract? | Repairs, insurance, taxes, interest, fees, depreciation |
| What's depreciation? | Building basis ÷ ~28 years/year |
| Repairs vs improvements? | Now- deductible vs over-years-deductible |
| Room in my own home? | Split expenses by square footage; <15 days is special |
| Who 1099s whom? | You 1099 contractors paid $600+; mgmt cos 1099 you |
| When do I sell? | Ask about gain, depreciation recapture (~25%), and exclusions |

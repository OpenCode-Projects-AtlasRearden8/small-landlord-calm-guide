# Chapter 10 — Key Dates (and the Companion Tracker)

Landlord trouble is, more often than not, a **calendar** problem wearing a
legal costume. This chapter lists the dates worth tracking and explains how
this manual pairs with our companion project, the
**lease-key-dates-tracker-webapp** (which tracks the *when*; this manual
teaches the *why*).

## The key-date list for any tenancy

| Date type | Why it matters | Typical lead time |
|---|---|---|
| Lease start / move-in day | Deposit clock, first rent, inspection | day-of |
| First rent due | Sets the payment rhythm | day-of |
| Lease end / renewal deadline | Auto-renewal clauses need advance notice from you | 60–90 days before |
| Notice deadline for a rent hike | Chapter 3 | 30–90 days before effective |
| Notice deadline for non-renewal / termination | State law sets the notice period | 30–60 days before |
| Deposit return deadline | Chapter 2's state deadline (14–30 day range) | day-of move-out + deadline |
| Inspection anniversaries | Drive-by, quarterly, photo refresh | every 3–6 months |
| Insurance renewal | Don't let a rental policy lapse silently | 30 days before |
| Property tax due dates | Budget item; escalate expense awareness | yearly |
| Smoke/CO alarm test dates | Every turnover and every new tenancy | turnover days |
| License/registration renewals | Some cities require rental registration or inspection | yearly/biennial |
| Tax filing (Schedule E) | Chapter 8 | April-ish, yearly |

Put **all** of these on a shared calendar the day a lease is signed. If your
memory is the kind that fails at exactly the wrong moment (that's most human
memory), the companion tracker exists so you don't have to be perfect.

## Working with the companion app

The **lease-key-dates-tracker-webapp** (a separate project) is built for
exactly this chapter's list. The pairing works like this:

- You **read** this manual to understand what a date means and why it exists
  (this chapter's table is the vocabulary).
- You **enter** the dates into the tracker with the lease's actual numbers —
  the tracker just counts down; it doesn't know your state's law, so enter
  the deadlines *you* verified (court self-help pages are the free source,
  per Chapter 1).
- The tracker **nudges** you with a lead time you choose (30 days is a fine
  default; use 90 for anything involving termination or a rent hike).

## The one habit that makes any of this work

When something happens that changes a date — the tenant pays late, the lease
amends, a repair promises a visit next week — **update the calendar the same
day.** A tracker that's a week stale is a tracker that's already lying to you.

## The annual review (twenty minutes, once a year)

Pick one date a year — the day you file taxes is a good one — and:

1. Confirm the tracker's dates still match the current lease(s).
2. Re-look-up the one or two state rules that changed (the AG's website,
   ten minutes).
3. Check the records folder (Chapter 7) for anything still missing.
4. Ask yourself the Chapter 9 question again: keep going, hire out, or sell?

## Date-entry checklist

- [ ] Every date type from the table above, for each unit?
- [ ] Lead times set: 90 days for legal-type notices, 30 for money-type?
- [ ] Calendar also visible to someone else (partner, friend) in emergencies?
- [ ] Updated the same day anything changed?

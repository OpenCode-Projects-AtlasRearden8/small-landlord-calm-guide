# Chapter 3 — Raising Rent (Kindly, Legally)

Rent hikes are where small landlords most often accidentally generate bad
feelings — not because a specific number was greedy, but because the *notice*
was late, or unexplained, or delivered by a text at 11pm.

> Education only, not legal advice. Notice periods, cap rules, and
> rent-control rules vary enormously by state and city. Some cities cap
> increases outright; some states require 60–90 days' notice for bigger hikes;
> some places require nothing beyond the lease terms. Look up your local rules
> every single time.

## The two rules underneath all of them

1. **The lease controls during the term.** If you signed a 12-month lease at
   $1,500, that's the rent until it ends (or you both agree, in writing, to a
   change). You generally don't get to hike mid-term.
2. **You must give written notice before a change takes effect.** The notice
   period (often 30, 60, or 90 days depending on the size of the hike and your
   local rules) is *law*, not a courtesy, in many places. And when it's only a
   courtesy, doing it properly is still the right thing.

That's the entire skeleton. Everything else is tone and paperwork.

## Sizing the increase: a round-numbers way to think

Your costs move — insurance, water, the tax bill, the roof someday. Rent
eventually has to follow. A **modest, explainable** increase tends to be worth
more to you than a big, resentment-generating one: motivated, long-term tenants
who pay on time and tell you about the leak on day one are genuinely the most
valuable asset a small landlord has.

Round-number example, for shape only:

> Your combined year costs on a rental went from about $9,000 to about
> $9,600. Your current rent of $1,500/month covers it, barely. Instead of
> contorting yourself into $1,537, a clean "$75/month, up 5%, starting after
> notice" is a number you can say out loud with a straight face, and a number
> your tenant can hear without rage.

It is almost never smart to raise rent *punishingly* the year after you've
raised it, or to raise mid-lease "because the market moved." It is very often
smart to raise rent a little, on a schedule, with a paragraph of explanation.

## When to send the notice

- Send early. Sixty days before the lease ends is a great default; more for
  bigger increases.
- Send in writing: email with a requested read receipt *and* a paper copy, or
  whatever your state prefers (some places require certified mail for
  termination-type notices — check).
- Include: the new rent, the effective date, a one-line reason ("combined
  utility and insurance costs rose about 6% this year"), and what happens if
  they don't want to renew (Chapter 10's dates list covers this end).

## A rent-increase letter you'd be glad to receive

> Dear [Name],
>
> I wanted to write before the lease renews so there are no surprises. Rent
> for [address] will go from $1,500 to $1,575 starting [date]. Combined
> costs — insurance, water, the tax bill — went up about 6% this year, and
> I'm not asking you to absorb all of that, just part.
>
> Nothing changes about the lease otherwise. If you'd like to talk about it,
> call or text me at [number]; if it truly doesn't work with your budget,
> I'd rather you tell me now than in month eleven, and I'll treat the
> situation fairly in referencing you onward if you move.
>
> Thanks for taking good care of the place.
> — [Your name]

Feel free to steal that. Notice it does three things: it names the number, it
gives one reason, and it treats the tenant as an adult who can make decisions.

## The three things not to do

1. **Don't surprise them.** A rent hike heard about on the due date guarantees
   an ugly conversation.
2. **Don't raise rent as a punishment or a negotiation tactic.** It reads as
   retaliation and, in many places, retaliation-adjacent behavior is legally
   tricky (retaliation rules do exist — see below).
3. **Don't skip the written record.** Verbal rent agreements create the
   worst kind of dispute: the one where both people believe they're right.

## Retaliation, briefly

In many states, raising rent (or threatening to) within a short window after a
tenant exercises a legal right — like reporting a code violation, joining a
tenant group, or requesting a repair — can be presumed retaliation. If your
tenant just stood up for a habitability right, this is exactly the wrong month
for a hike. Wait, explain, and act at a normal, non-linked time.

## Rent control / rent stabilization, briefly

Some cities (and a growing number of states) limit annual increases — sometimes
with an annual allowed percentage, sometimes only for certain building ages or
sizes, sometimes with exemptions for ADUs or owner-occupied duplexes. If you
rent in a regulated place, your city's housing department usually has a
one-page guide. Read it before you pick a number, not after.

## When you *shouldn't* raise rent at all

- When the tenant has been there a long time and the current rent still works
  for you — the value of a reliable, happy long-term tenant often beats a
  theoretical market rent you'd have to chase.
- When you're charging *below* market on purpose (family-friend, long-time
  housemate, an arrangement you're satisfied with). Write the agreed number
  down once, and review it with them every couple of years if you both want.
- When the relationship is carrying your otherwise-quiet tenancy. Peace is a
  line item with a real dollar value.

## Rent-hike checklist

- [ ] Lease allows the change (or the term is ending)?
- [ ] Local notice period looked up and met?
- [ ] Amount sized so you can explain it in one sentence?
- [ ] Written notice includes effective date and a contact?
- [ ] Sent early — 60 days, more for big jumps?
- [ ] Copy saved in your records with send date and method?
- [ ] No retaliation window conflict?

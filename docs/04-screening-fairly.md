# Chapter 4 — Screening Applicants Fairly

Screening is where education pays off most — because mistakes here aren't just
unfair, they can be *illegal* in ways that cost real money and real lawsuits.

> Education only, not legal advice. Fair housing law is federal, state, and
> local, and there are more protected classes at the state and local level than
> the federal ones. Your city or state may add marital status, source of
> income, age, or others. Look up "[your state] fair housing protected
> classes" before you write an ad or a form.

## The federal floor (HUD's seven)

You may not discriminate based on:

1. Race
2. Color
3. National origin
4. Religion
5. Sex (including sexual orientation and gender identity under current
   federal interpretation)
6. Familial status (having kids, being pregnant)
7. Disability

State and local laws frequently add: age, marital status, source of income
(including Section 8/housing vouchers in many cities and states), military
status, immigration status, domestic violence survivor status, and others.

**Practical implication:** you're not allowed to reject someone because of
*who they are*. You are allowed to evaluate *how they'll be as a tenant* —
using the same measuring stick for everyone.

## The one rule that makes all of this simpler

**Same criteria, same questions, same order, for every applicant.**

Write your criteria down *before* you list the place (not when you're staring
at an application). A calm example set you could use:

- Combined gross income of **around 3× monthly rent** (round number, and many
  landlords prefer 2.5–3×).
- No evictions for unpaid rent or lease violations within (say) the last
  **5 years**, or however your standard reads. (Careful: in some places
  recent-eviction checks are restricted or require explanation; look it up.)
- Rent or mortgage history showing on-time payments for a stated period.
- Positive reference from the most recent landlord (if any).
- Named pets must fit a stated pet policy (with the assistance-animal carve-out
  below).
- Willing to sign the standard lease as written (or negotiate only before
  signing, not after).

Then evaluate each applicant by that same list. Score it privately on paper.
Say no only by pointing at the window in the list: "the income ratio wasn't
there," "the most recent landlord reference didn't say positive," and so on.
That's how *fair* and *defensible* end up meaning the same thing.

## The question list you ask everyone

- Full name(s) of every adult who'll live there.
- Current employer(s) and how long there.
- Monthly income (screenshot of two recent paystubs or similar proof).
- Current and prior landlord (name, phone/email, how long they lived there).
- Any pets (and assistance animals will come up later as a *different* thing).
- Any eviction filings in the look-back you've chosen — asked the same way
  for everyone.
- Emergency contact.
- "Anything you'd like me to know?" — the kindest question in the whole list.
  Applicants with a rough patch and a clean story will tell you, and often
  the *explanation* is the same thing a credit-score exception would have
  told you.

## What never to ask

- Age, or "do you have / plan to have kids?"
- Religion, national origin, race, the shape of anyone's family.
- "Where are you from?" (either version of that question).
- "Is this a Section 8 voucher?" *if your area's source-of-income laws apply*
  — check locally; in many places refusing vouchers outright is illegal.
- Whether someone is disabled, or details of any medical situation.

If you catch yourself asking a question to *size a person up* rather than to
*evaluate tenancy*, stop and rewrite the question.

## Credit and background checks

- Use a reputable tenant-screening service, charge only your actual cost (and
  only what your jurisdiction allows), and only after they've applied.
- Get **written consent** — the screening service will make this a click, but
  you keep the record.
- Use the *same service* for everyone, so the data you see is comparable.
- A low credit score alone is rarely the whole story; a credit report showing
  stable rent payments and one old medical bill is a different human than a
  report full of unpaid rent judgments.

## Assistance animals are not pets

If an applicant (or tenant) presents a documented assistance animal — service
animal or emotional support animal, particularly in a housing context — the
animal is not a pet under fair housing rules. You normally:

- don't count it against pet limits,
- don't charge pet rent or pet deposit for *that* animal (damage by any animal
  can still be charged to the tenant's regular obligations),
- treat the request like any other reasonable-accommodation request: ask for
  the documentation you'd ask anyone for, in the same way, and respond in
  writing.

Never demand to see medical records. A housing letter from a treating
professional is the normal documentation; look up HUD's guidance on assistance
animals for the specifics.

## Picking someone, gently

You are allowed to prefer a good applicant over a merely acceptable one.
You are not allowed to prefer one *kind of person* over another.

A short decision memo is worth writing even for a rental: *"Applicant A met
all six criteria; applicant B missed the income ratio; chose A on [date]."*
Two sentences. It's your memory when your memory fails.

Once you choose, tell the *runner-ups* politely — it's generous, it's rare,
and it means if your first choice falls through, your second choice remembers
you kindly: *"We really appreciated your application; we've gone with another
applicant whose income ratio fit the criteria more closely. We'd be glad to
keep your application on file this cycle if the first placement doesn't
complete."*

## Showing the place: the practical part

- Schedule consistent times for everyone, prefer a few group showings, and
  always have someone (a friend, a partner) at the showing for a room in
  your own home.
- Hand everyone the same one-page sheet: rent, deposit, lease length,
  pet/guest rules, and your criteria so they can *self*-screen. Applicants who
  self-reject are applicants you didn't have to seem heartless rejecting.
- Tell people honestly about the unit's flaws (the stairs, the shared
  driveway, the train at 6:40am). Nobody moves in resentful.

## Screening checklist

- [ ] Written criteria exist **before** the first inquiry?
- [ ] Every applicant gets the same questions, in the same order?
- [ ] No protected-class questions anywhere (ad included)?
- [ ] Credit/background check only after application + written consent?
- [ ] Same screening provider used for everyone?
- [ ] Assistance-animal requests handled as accommodations, not pets?
- [ ] Chosen applicant documented with a two-sentence memo?
- [ ] All application records filed (Chapter 7), including the rejects?

# Chapter 6 — Repair Requests, Handled Kindly

A dripping faucet is not a repair request. It's a **test of who you are as a
landlord.** Tenants remember two things about you: how fast the fix came, and
whether you were decent while it was happening.

> Education only, not legal advice. States and cities set the actual
> habitability standards (heat, water, electricity, structure, working locks,
> mold-free, pest-free, working smoke/CO alarms) and actual repair timelines
> (many say things like "reasonable time," with some defining quick deadlines
> for essential services). Look up your state's repair-and-habitability
> rules so you know where "soon" actually lands.

## The repair ladder

Every request falls somewhere on a ladder. Sort it before you panic about cost:

1. **Emergency** — no water, no heat in winter, active leak flooding, no
   working fridge/freeze, live electrical hazard, gas smell, a broken lock or
   window that makes the home unsecure, fire or CO alarm chirping and
   undeterminable.
   *Your move in minutes, not days. Call now, drive now.*
2. **Urgent (a few days)** — one fixture busted (one sink won't drain),
   appliance on its way out, a pest sighting, a running toilet you can hear
   from outside, a crack in a window.
   *Same week is your target.*
3. **Routine (the week after)** — slow drain, a paint scuff, a sticky door,
   a lightbulb in a vaulted ceiling, a blind slat.
   *Say a date; hit the date.*
4. **Cosmetic / future project** — "the hallway paint is 2004 beige."
   *Honest "not yet, here's when" beats a silent maybe.*

If you're not sure, one clarifying question and one photo usually sorts it.
*"Can you text a photo of it? If it's the fitting at the base, I can likely
swap the part myself Thursday; if the cabinet base is swollen, that's a pro,
and I'll call one this week."*

## The three-part reply (in writing, always)

When you get a repair text, reply the same day with these three things:

1. **Acknowledge** — "Got it. That's on my list as of today."
2. **Triage** — "Sounds like routine, not emergency. Correct me if I'm wrong
   if you come home from work and the kitchen's flooding."
3. **A date** — "I'll be there Thursday at 5, or my plumber will, and he'll
   text you a window."

That's it. A reply like that takes two minutes and buys more goodwill than
anything else a small landlord does. Silence is the actual enemy — tenants
tolerate slow repairs far better than no-idea repairs.

## Two scripts for the two awkward moments

**When your tenant caused it:**

> "Looked at it — this looks like the drain stopper got something jammed in
> it. Happy to get the part out; if it was something like a toy or a fork,
> fair to ask we share that. Same price either way roughly; I just want to
> understand so it doesn't happen again. Either way I'll get it draining
> today."

Notice the shape: no accusation, no lecture, one question so they can own it
or not.

**When you caused it (or you're not sure yet):**

> "You've gotten water 3 out of the last 4 Tuesdays and I can't figure out why
> from here. I'm paying a plumber to come look — I'll text you the visit
> window and keep you posted. Sorry you all are dealing with this."

The words *I'll keep you posted* are among the most valuable a landlord owns.

## DIY vs. pro: an honest bar

A small landlord can comfortably DIY (or have a favorite handyperson do):

- swapping a faucet washer or a fill valve
- smoke-detector batteries and obvious exterior caulk
- furnace filters and drugstore-level pest traps
- a screen patch, a door hinge, a stuck garbage disposal button
- a light fixture swap when the breaker's off and you truly know how

Hire out, always:

- anything involving the breaker box, the water heater, or the roof
- gas lines (ever, forever, no)
- structural anything, anything with mold under a surface, tree work
- sewer/septic backups
- your own time when it's 11pm on a work night and the answer is a plumber
- and frankly: if you've never done the job before in daylight, don't start
  after dark.

Round-number rule: if a pro would be less than two hours' labor, it's usually
cheaper to just call the pro than it is to buy back the weekend you lose
fumbling.

## To-do if you can't fix it right away

Life happens — you're traveling, work is chaos, the part is on a truck
somewhere. The move is **not silence.** A tenant waiting on a fix with no
news is a tenant running a little calculator in their head headed toward
rent withholding or a housing-department call as a *last resort* (real
rights, real complicated, worth never triggering). Update them even when
there's no update:

> "Still waiting on the vendor's estimate — they promised today or tomorrow
> morning. Thursday morning I'll text you the install slot no matter what."

## Records to save from every repair

1. The original request, and your first reply (timestamp is the whole point).
2. Photos before and after.
3. The invoice or receipt.
4. A one-line note of what the actual cause was.

That's the repair file. Chapter 7 keeps it.

## A friendly word about "kind" — and why it's also smart

Kindness in repairs is not a personality badge. It's the mechanism by which
tenants *tell you early* about the small leaks. The landlord who yells when
asked to look at a slow drain teaches their tenant not to mention the slow
drain — and then inherits the ceiling repair that the slow drain became.

Your reputation at the town hardware store is worth real money. Be the one
they know: pay the good contractors fast, say thank you, and you'll be firmly
at the front of the emergency list the January your water heater dies.

## Repair-request checklist

- [ ] Reply the same day (acknowledge, triage, give a date)?
- [ ] Emergency numbers saved in the tenant's welcome sheet?
- [ ] Everything in writing somewhere it can't vanish (text/email/print)?
- [ ] Invoices, receipts, before/afters filed in the repair folder?
- [ ] DIY/pro line drawn honestly?
- [ ] Tenant updated when there's no update?

# Chapter 1 — The Lease, Decoded

A lease is a contract, but it's mostly a *schedule of promises* wrapped in
nineteenth-century vocabulary. This chapter walks the standard sections one by
one and tells you what each is actually saying.

> Education only, not legal advice. Your state's required lease language may
> differ; some states mandate specific disclosures (like flood zones, mold, or
> registered sex offender databases) in writing. Look up "[your state]
> residential lease required disclosures" and read your attorney general's or
> court self-help page first.

## Where to get a lease for free

You do not need to pay for a lease. Good free sources, in order:

1. **Court self-help / judicial branch websites.** Many states publish a
   plain-language residential lease or required-notice forms as free PDFs.
2. **State attorney general tenant guides.** Usually include model language.
3. **HUD and federal publications** for the federal side (fair housing, lead
   paint disclosure — federally required for pre-1978 buildings).

What you should *never* do is copy a lease from another state's forum post.
Required clauses vary wildly by state.

## The standard lease, clause by clause

### 1. Parties and premises

Say *exactly* who is renting (name the adults who will live there) and *exactly
what* they're renting (full address, unit letter, and — for an ADU — the legal
description of what counts as the unit, e.g., "the rear detached structure
including the bedroom, kitchenette, and the private yard bounded by the fence").

**Why it matters:** Someone who never signed the lease isn't on it. Someone who
did sign is jointly responsible for the whole rent and the whole mess. Do not
let a partner "just move in" informally — either add them to the lease or handle it with a written amendment.

### 2. Term (how long)

Fixed term ("12 months, starting March 1") vs. periodic ("month-to-month").
Fixed terms give both sides certainty; month-to-month gives flexibility.
Calm default for a first-time small landlord: 12 months, then switch to
month-to-month unless both people want to renew.

Watch for the word **automatic renewal** — in some places it requires advance
written notice from *you* months ahead. Put renewal deadlines on your calendar
(the key-dates tracker exists exactly for this).

### 3. Rent: amount, due date, and grace period

Most leases say rent is due on the 1st with a small grace period (often "3
days" in leases everywhere — in practice your state's law may or may not give
one automatically; check). Say the *amount in dollars and cents*, the accepted
payment methods, and where payments go.

**Decoded:** money terms. "Rent" = the base amount. "Late fee" in many states
must be a reasonable estimate of your actual cost — some states cap it or
require it be genuine, not profit. Free-standing rule: a late fee you negotiated
down to a tiny number is kinder than a big scary one you never enforce.

### 4. Security deposit

Amount, what it covers, and — critically — a pointer to the deposit rules
(Chapter 2). Some states cap deposits as a multiple of rent (for example,
"not more than two months' rent"). If your state caps, your lease can't exceed
the cap even if both of you agreed to more.

### 5. Utilities and responsibilities

Who pays electricity, gas, water, sewer, trash, internet, and who mows, shovels,
and replaces lightbulbs. For a room rental, be specific: is the tenant sharing
*your* utilities account, and how is their share computed (say a round number
like "half of the combined bill, averaged over the year, $X per month")?

### 6. Repairs and maintenance

This clause usually says the tenant reports problems and doesn't damage things,
and you fix things. Chapter 6 covers how that should actually run day to day.
Never (in almost any state) may a lease waive your duty to keep the place
habitable — a clause saying the tenant "accepts the premises as-is, all repairs
waived" is often void. Don't try to negotiate habitability away.

### 7. Entry and privacy

Typical language: you may enter with "reasonable notice" (often defined as
about a day, in many states 24–48 hours) except in emergencies. Write your
notice in *hours* in your lease so it's specific. Add a note about
non-emergency but time-sensitive access, like a plumber appointment.

### 8. Pets, guests, and smoking

- **Guests** — define when a guest becomes an occupant (a classic measure:
  more than 14 days in any 30, or 7 consecutive). Without a definition,
  "guests" become unwritten roommates.
- **Pets** — if you allow them, name the specific animals ("one cat, named").
  Note that in many places, documented assistance animals are *not* pets and
  can't be charged pet rent or deposits. Chapter 4 covers this.
- **Smoking** — say where it's allowed, or say nowhere.

### 9. Alterations

"Paint the walls" and "install a ceiling fan" and "run wires for a home office"
are all alterations. Say what needs written permission versus what's fine.

### 10. Subletting and assignment

For small landlords, the answer is usually "not without written consent." This
prevents your tenant from quietly becoming *your* landlord's problem.

### 11. Default and remedies

This is the "what happens if things go wrong" clause: notice to cure
(a chance to fix it), termination, and the landlord's right to evict.
What it says in scary language is simple: if X goes wrong, there's a process —
and the process is (in every U.S. state) **the court, not you changing the
locks**. "Self-help eviction" (shutting off power, removing doors, tossing
belongings without a court order) is illegal almost everywhere, even when the
tenant is clearly in the wrong.

### 12. Quiet enjoyment and house rules

"Quiet enjoyment" does not mean silence. It means the tenant's right to use the
place without the landlord lurking or showing up. House rules (laundry hours,
shared-space schedules, bins out by Tuesday) live here.

### 13. Lead paint and required disclosures

If your building was built before 1978, federal law requires a **lead-based
paint disclosure** pamphlet and form in the lease packet. This is one of the
few federally-required lease attachments for small rentals. Free form, free
pamphlet, from HUD.

### 14. Signatures and dates

Every adult who lives there signs. Every page. Date it. Keep a signed copy for
each signer and one for yourself. If you change the lease later, use a written
**lease amendment** both parties sign — not a text message.

## A little decoder ring: suspicious-sounding contract words

| Lease phrase | In plain words |
|---|---|
| "Premises" | The place itself |
| "Lessor / lessee" | Landlord / tenant |
| "In witness whereof" | "You agree and so do I" |
| "Time is of the essence" | Dates really matter |
| "Holdover" | Staying past your lease end |
| "Condemn" | Government declares the place unusable |
| "Severability" | If one clause is illegal, the rest still stands |
| "Waiver" | Choosing not to enforce something — once |
| "Indemnify" | "You cover my costs if..." (this one you should read closely) |

## The one-page pre-signing checklist

- [ ] Every adult on the lease? (Not "oh, he'll sign later.")
- [ ] Rent amount, due date, and grace period in plain numbers?
- [ ] Deposit amount and no cap violation?
- [ ] Who pays each utility written down, not implied?
- [ ] Repairs: who handles what, and a phone number/email for you?
- [ ] Entry notice hours specified?
- [ ] Pet/guest/smoking rules stated — including assistance-animal carve-out?
- [ ] Lead paint disclosure attached, if pre-1978?
- [ ] Any local-required disclosures attached?
- [ ] Signed, dated, and every signer walks out with a copy?

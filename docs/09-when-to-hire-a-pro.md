# Chapter 9 — When to Hire a Pro (or Sell to a Property Company)

Most small landlords never need a property manager, and none need to feel bad
about their choice either way. This chapter gives you the decision points, the
round-number costs, and the exit options — including selling the whole thing
to an investor.

> Education only, not legal or financial or tax advice. Fees and market
> practices vary by region. Numbers here are round and illustrative.

## The honest question underneath it all

Landlording is a job — two or three hours a month in a good year, plus
emergencies. Whether to keep doing that job yourself isn't a question of
*capability*. It's a question of *season*:

- a new baby, a new shift, a parent's illness
- a second rental (you doubled the work, not the joy)
- moving out of town
- the January your tenant stops paying and you realize you'd rather not spend
  your Sunday nights studying eviction paperwork

Any of those is a legitimate reason to hire help. None of them is failure.

## Option A: hire a property manager

**Round-number cost:** 8–10% of the monthly rent for ongoing management, plus
often 50–100% of one month's rent to find a tenant, plus occasional markups on
maintenance. For a $1,500 rent, that's roughly **$120–$150 a month**, or about
$1,500–$1,800 a year.

**What you get:** rent collection, contractor coordination, tenant
communication, late-notices filed properly, vacancy filling. **What you give
up:** some control, some profit, and the immediate knowledge of what's
happening in your own house.

**How to evaluate a property manager:**

1. Ask what's included in the monthly fee (entry paperwork? inspections?
   renewals?) and in the leasing fee.
2. Ask how they handle repairs over a dollar amount you set (many landlords
   set a $300–$500 approval threshold — below it the manager spends, above it
   they call you). Put your threshold in the contract, not in a handshake.
3. Ask how often they inspect and what's sent to you (you want a report with
   photos, twice a year at minimum).
4. Ask for their owner statements and see if you can *read* one.
5. Read the management agreement for how either side exits — 30 days' notice
   is normal; a punitive auto-renewal is a sign to keep shopping.
6. Check the license requirement in your state (some states license property
   managers under real-estate law) and ask for the license or the number and
   check it.

**You still** own the lease (or a copy of it), deposit decisions, tax filings,
and the bank account. Keep your records folder going (Chapter 7) even with a
manager — their statements will be one input into your records, not a
replacement.

**When it's clearly worth it:** you're remote, or you simply keep not
sleeping. **When it isn't:** one tenant, stable, local, pays on autopay, and
you handle a repair and a rent hike each year without drama.

## Option B: partial help instead

Between DIY and full management there are à-la-carte services:

- **Tenant placement only** (finder's fee, "we find them; you manage them")
  — good for finding applicants when you hate listing/screening.
- **A handyman on retainer** — one monthly fee or a preferred-vendor list,
  for the "sink dripping" tier (Chapter 6's ladder, tier 3).
- **Bookeeping help** — a bookkeeper's spreadsheet each quarter so tax season
  is calm (roughly $50–$150/month for one property, depending).
- **Structure help** — a one-time lawyer consult ($200–$500) to review your
  lease once, or your LLC question, before any crisis.

You can cobble together exactly the help you lack, which is often cheaper and
calmer than all-or-nothing management.

## Option C: sell to a property company

Sometimes the right answer is **exit** — sell the rental itself, possibly to
a real-estate investor or a "we buy houses" type company.

**What that offer looks like:** investor buyers pay you fast, with little
signing drama, but often at a noticeably discounted price (it isn't uncommon
for offers to land tens of thousands lower than what a normal listing might
fetch). You're trading some money for certainty and speed, and you're
specifically *not* paying for repairs, showings, and a listing period.

**Round-number sanity check before you accept an offer:**

1. Get a comparative opinion (2–3 normal agent contacts or a local broker's
   opinion) of what a standard listing would realistically fetch; so
   you know what discount you're actually accepting.
2. Estimate your net after normal sale costs (agent commission, seller's
   closing costs) — and honestly compare that against the investor's offer
   *net to you*.
3. Remember the tax picture from Chapter 8 (depreciation recapture, and the
   main-home exclusion if it ever was your home). A CPA consult (the
   $300–$800 kind) *before* you sign anything is money well spent.
4. Get any offer in writing, with a deposit amount, a timeline, and what's
   "as-is" — and have a real-estate attorney or agent review it if the number
   is meaningful to your life.
5. Mention the **lease**: a property sold with a tenant attached transfers the
   lease (that's called "subject to the lease"), which is fine — but the
   buyer should know what exists (the lease, the deposit they'd inherit, the
   security deposit you owe on). Investors expecting owner-occupied homes who
   didn't know about the lease will renegotiate at the worst moment.

**When it's worth it:** you're moving out of town, the property is wearing
you down, the market's hot, or you need liquidity for whatever the next
chapter of your life actually is. Selling is allowed to be the right answer
for reasons other than profit.

## Option D: do nothing, keep going

Also sometimes right. If the current arrangement is fine and no season of
your life is asking you to change it, no decision is *required*. Reviewing
annually (put it in Chapter 10's calendar) is plenty.

## A calm decision matrix

| Situation | Leaning |
|---|---|
| Moving out of town within a year | Manager, or sell if you're converting cash |
| Second property on the same street | Manage both; efficiency comes free |
| One stable tenant, you like the work | Stay DIY, review annually |
| Recurring dread, conflict, or sleep loss | Manager — the fee is cheaper than the dread |
| Big repair looming (roof, furnace) | One-time consults; sell only if the numbers agree |
| Health/family season shift | Manager or partial help; no guilt in any direction |

## And one last thing

The reason this guide stays education-only is that this decision — keep, hire
out, sell — is a **life** decision first, money second. Whatever you choose
next spring or three years from now, the paperwork and the habits in this
manual are what make any of the options possible. Records by address. Lease
by clauses. Photos by dates. Onward.

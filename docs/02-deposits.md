# Chapter 2 — Deposits

The security deposit is the classic first-landlord's headache. The rules are
simple in shape even when they differ in the details from state to state.

> Education only, not legal advice. Deposit rules (caps, deadlines, interest,
> separate accounts) are **highly** state-specific and change often. Look up
> your state's tenant guide before you set or spend a deposit.

## What a deposit actually is

It isn't "the tenant's money I keep for costs." In most states it legally
remains the tenant's money that you're *holding* until the tenancy ends. That
one idea explains half the rules you'll find confusing:

- you may owe **interest** on it in some states,
- you may need to keep it in a **separate account** (in a few states, even an
  interest-bearing one, sometimes at a local bank),
- you have a hard **deadline** to return it after move-out,
- you must return it **with an itemized list** of any deductions.

## The four questions that vary by state

| Question | Round-number example of what states say |
|---|---|
| How much can I charge? | "No cap," "1 month," "2x monthly rent," or more for pets/renewals |
| How fast must I return it? | Ranges around 14–30 days after the tenant moves out |
| Do I need an itemized list? | Almost always yes, in writing, for every deduction |
| Do I owe interest? | Some states yes (sometimes only for larger buildings or deposits) |

**Find your state's version of this table before your first tenancy** — your
state AG or court self-help site will have it in plain English for free.

## Picking a fair deposit amount

For a room in your own home, a common calm default is one month's rent. For a
house or ADU, one month is normal; some landlords add a modest (say $200–300)
pet deposit when a named pet moves in.

Two things a deposit is *for*: unpaid rent (last month), damage beyond normal
wear. Two things it is *not* for, in most places:

- **normal wear and tear** — carpet that wore through by foot traffic, faded
  paint, scuffed walls, an old appliance that died of old age;
- **upgrades** — the tenant's deposit doesn't buy you nicer carpet.

A helpful mental test for a deduction: *"Would I have had to pay for this
anyway if the tenant hadn't been here? Then it's probably not deductible."*

## Holding the money

Three habits that save entire arguments later:

1. Keep deposit money **separate** from your spending money (a separate bank
   sub-account is fine — it doesn't need to be complicated).
2. Log the deposit in your records (Chapter 7) with date received, amount, and
   where it's held.
3. Do **not** spend the deposit mid-tenancy on a repair "planning to pay
   myself back." If your state requires a separate/special account, spending
   it can be a violation by itself.

## Returning the deposit: the calm procedure

When the tenant moves out (Chapter 5 has the move-out checklist):

1. **Do the move-out inspection within the first day or two** of keys coming
   back — not weeks later.
2. **Itemize everything** you plan to deduct: what was removed, why it's more
   than normal wear, and the actual invoice or receipt for each item. When you
   don't yet have an invoice (say, the carpet pro hasn't quoted you), many
   landlords send a written "estimate, final numbers to follow" note inside
   the legal deadline.
3. **Mail or otherwise deliver it** the way your state requires (some say
   certified mail to a forwarding address), and return the net amount.
4. **Copy everything into your records** — the inspection photos, the
   itemized list, the receipt proving you returned the money.

Round-number example (not law, just arithmetic): tenant paid $1,200 deposit,
move-out finds a broken window ($150 replacement, from your glass invoice) and
one day of unreturned trash bins with a note requiring removal ($0 — too small
to be a fair deduction). Itemized list: minus $150 for the window with
invoice attached; refund $1,050 by check or the bank method your state prefers,
*within your state's deadline* (often in the 14–30 day range).

## When the tenant disagrees

It happens. The calm path:

1. Send the itemized list with receipts before they ask.
2. If they dispute an item, ask for their account in writing and respond in
   writing — specifically, calmly, and with the same photos you used.
3. If you can't agree, small claims court is where deposit disputes go. If
   you keep good records (Chapter 7), you'll wish you'd had that conversation
   earlier, because the paperwork will do most of the arguing for you.

One advantage worth repeating: **a deposit dispute you can document
usually resolves itself.** It's the undocumented one that gets expensive.

## Room-in-your-own-home special case

If you're renting a room *in the home you also live in* (the classic "housemate"
situation), several states treat the arrangement a little differently — fewer
protections apply to you, and fewer apply to them. Still: pick an amount, put
it in writing, and follow the same return-with-itemization habit. Informal does
not mean undocumented.

## Deposit checklist

- [ ] Know your state's cap, deadline, and interest rules (AG or court site).
- [ ] Keep deposit funds separate, logged, and unspent.
- [ ] Charge an amount you can explain in one sentence.
- [ ] At move-out: inspect fast, itemize every deduction with a receipt or note.
- [ ] Return the net amount within your state's deadline, using your state's
      required delivery method.
- [ ] File the whole packet (photos, list, refund proof) in your records.
